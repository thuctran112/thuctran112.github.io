## The problem
EuroCTP needed a single website serving several distinct audiences at once: explaining who the organization is, detailing services for institutional clients, explaining how the consolidated tape works, sharing production status, and providing market data and education to a retail audience with future revenue potential through advertising and paid features. In total, the site had to serve around seven distinct personas, ranging from retail investors with no financial background to institutional operators, academics, and regulators, across multiple European languages and countries. The business stakes were real: the site needed to build credibility and support the organization's positioning ahead of ESMA's official selection of the consolidated tape provider, within a tight initial investment.

## The approach
Rather than user research, this project relied on an audit: working alongside a UX researcher, I went through existing materials and stakeholder requirements to clarify constraints across personas, then defined the information architecture and built a first version of the website's structure and dashboard.

## My contribution
- Audited existing materials and stakeholder requirements to shape the site's information architecture
- Designed the site's core structure end-to-end, including the homepage, dashboard, and instrument page
- Prioritized design investment across personas under a fixed budget and timeline
- Delivered a shipped, client-approved website by the end of the engagement

## Key decisions

**Prioritizing design investment by strategic stakes, not by page count.** The client set a specific constraint: ESMA was expected to evaluate the site primarily through the eyes of the least sophisticated retail persona, while EuroCTP's actual revenue was expected to come from professional and institutional users. My job was to translate that given constraint into where design effort actually went: the welcome page and instrument page, which carried the core "consolidated tape" value proposition and were most visible to both evaluators and users, got the most design investment, while other sections used a more templated treatment.

**Separate paths for different audiences, with a shared complexity toggle.** Rather than building distinct experiences for retail, institutional, and professional users end to end, I designed separate top-level navigation destinations for institutional-specific content (like services and self-service portal access), while keeping the core market-data pages shared. A complexity toggle let the same instrument page scale from a simple retail view up to full professional-grade density, instead of maintaining two versions of the same page.

**Designing within a sustainability constraint.** The client required the site to minimize its carbon footprint, a real tension given the product's core value was auto-refreshing, real-time market data. I designed configurable options (adjustable refresh rates, a dark mode) so users could reduce their own data consumption without losing the real-time value proposition by default.

## The solution
- **Homepage and dashboard**: core structure designed to orient multiple distinct audiences, prioritized as the site's primary surface
- **Instrument page**: the site's most data-dense screen, built with a complexity toggle so it could serve both retail and professional users from one structure
- **Real-time data interactions**: designed color-coded update patterns for live market data changes, aiming for a distinctive, delightful feel within tight performance and "green IT" constraints
- **Supporting screens**: covering the site's other primary purposes (institutional services, production status, market data, education)
- Designed to WCAG 2.2 accessibility standards throughout, and built core data components with future reuse in mind, as the foundation for a possible future paid "trading dashboard" product

## Outcome
The project shipped and was handed off at the end of the client engagement, with strong positive feedback from EuroCTP.

## Reflection
Looking back, I'd have pushed for user research and usability testing if budget allowed. The design was based on stakeholder requirements and assumptions rather than direct user testing.
